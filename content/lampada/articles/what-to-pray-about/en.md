Mount Athos, early in the twentieth century. Silouan, an Orthodox monk who had once been a peasant in central Russia, was made steward of his monastery. In the mornings he made the rounds of the workshops, but gave the foremen only general instructions and then went back to his cell to weep for “God’s people”. He had as many as two hundred workmen in his charge[^corpus-silouan-workshop]. His disciple, Archimandrite Sophrony, recorded in his book about the elder how Silouan spoke of these men: “Here is Mikhail, who left his wife and children back in the village and works here for a pittance”[^corpus-silouan-mikhail]. Then Nikita, newly married, who had left a pregnant wife and an old mother at home, and Grigory, who had left his parents, his wife and two little ones to earn a crust of bread[^corpus-silouan-nikita]. “Here is Nikolka, still just a boy…”[^corpus-silouan-nikolka]

If you don’t know what to pray about, start the same way: with what is in your life today. The people you saw, the task that worries you, what you are grateful for and where you went wrong. There is no single correct list of things to pray for; you can tell God about anything. But if you want something to hold on to, Christians have long used a few “maps”: the people around you, the parts of prayer (praise, thanksgiving, confession, asking, praying for others) and the Lord’s Prayer. Here is how four very different people used them.

## “Here is Mikhail…”

Semyon Antonov was born into a peasant family in 1866 and came to Athos in 1892[^corpus-silouan-formular]. Back in his village, on the feast day of the local church, he ran into a tipsy cobbler, a huge man and a notorious brawler, who mockingly tried to grab Semyon’s accordion[^corpus-silouan-feast]. Semyon didn’t back down. He struck the bully in the chest so hard that the man went flying and crashed onto his back in the middle of the road, and Semyon froze in horror, sure he had killed him[^corpus-silouan-hit]. That young man grew into a monk of whom his disciple would write that he “prayed a great prayer for the whole world”[^corpus-silouan-world].

Back to the two hundred workmen. He grieved, too, for the mothers and fathers who had sent a boy off with a heavy heart to live among strangers for a beggar’s wage, and over the fact that nobody cared what became of these poor people[^corpus-silouan-poverty]. “The Lord pities everyone”[^corpus-silouan-pity], he used to say. And also: “To pray for people is to shed blood”[^corpus-silouan-blood].

In his own writings Silouan advises praying even for the stubborn ones who won’t do as they are told: “Lord, give Your servant understanding: You love him”[^corpus-silouan-disobedient].

That gives us the first and simplest topic: the people you saw today. You don’t need to know what they need. It is enough to ask yourself how they are doing right now, and to tell God about it.

## A prayer list and a map

Robert Murray M’Cheyne was the Presbyterian minister of St Peter’s Church in Dundee, Scotland[^mccheyne]. Not long before his death he drew up a private review of everything in his life that needed to change[^corpus-mccheyne-amend], and he headed these notes “Reformation”[^corpus-mccheyne-reformation]. His friend Andrew Bonar, also a minister, wrote the *Memoir of Robert Murray M’Cheyne* after his death and printed the notes in full[^corpus-mccheyne-entire].

In the section on prayer, M’Cheyne starts with a rule: “I ought not to omit any of the parts of prayer—confession, adoration, thanksgiving, petition, and intercession.”[^corpus-mccheyne-parts] Then he owns up to the parts he skips. Adoration drops out “when I forget to whom I am speaking”[^corpus-mccheyne-adoration]. Thanksgiving fares no better: “I have the native tendency of the heart to omit giving thanks.”[^corpus-mccheyne-thanks] And praying for others goes too: “Often when the heart is selfish, dead to the salvation of others”[^corpus-mccheyne-selfish]. His conclusion is sober: “Perhaps every prayer need not have all these; but surely a day should not pass without some space being devoted to each.”[^corpus-mccheyne-each-day]

Then comes an actual prayer list. On it are his family and friends and, from his congregation, the sick and the grieving, the poor and the rich, believers and the indifferent, teachers and children. All of these he meant to pray for every day, along with shorter prayers for the whole town, the Church of Scotland, fellow ministers by name and missionaries among Jews and Gentiles[^corpus-mccheyne-list]. “It would stir me up to pray with the map before me.”[^corpus-mccheyne-map] And: “I must have a scheme of prayer, also the names of missionaries marked on the map.”[^corpus-mccheyne-scheme] He considered giving the different circles different days of the week, yet his family and congregation were to be prayed for daily[^corpus-mccheyne-days].

Ordinary tasks became topics as well. M’Cheyne noticed that he answered letters, and rushed off to see a sick parishioner, without first asking God, and he came to believe nothing should be done without prayer. He rested this on the words of the apostle Paul[^corpus-mccheyne-everything]: “Be anxious for nothing, but in everything, by prayer and petition, with thanksgiving, present your requests to God” (Philippians 4:6)[^phil4]. And for drowsiness at evening prayer he has advice that still works: the best remedy, he writes, is “planning what things I am to ask”[^corpus-mccheyne-plan].

The notes break off. Bonar remarks that M’Cheyne evidently never finished them[^corpus-mccheyne-unfinished]. Typhus was raging in his parish at the time, and, worn out as he was, he kept going to the bedsides of the sick[^corpus-mccheyne-typhus]. He was only twenty-nine when he died[^corpus-mccheyne-age]. His list is not bookkeeping. It is a way of not forgetting the people we usually forget.

## The prayer that already holds every topic

Simone Weil, the French philosopher, was drawn to Catholicism but declined baptism and stayed on the threshold of the Church; there is only an unconfirmed account that a friend baptized her shortly before she died. In 1942, as she was preparing to leave France, she wrote a long letter to a priest who was her friend, and it later became part of her book *Waiting for God*[^weil]. In it she admits: “Until last September I had never once prayed in my life, at least not in the literal sense of the word.”[^corpus-weil-never] She tells him she could only bring herself to share all this because she was leaving, and leaving with the thought that she would probably die[^corpus-weil-leaving].

The change began with the Lord’s Prayer. The summer before, while studying Greek with an acquaintance, she had gone through it word by word with him and they had promised each other to learn it by heart; a few weeks later she decided a promise was a promise and did it[^corpus-weil-promise]. “The infinite sweetness of this Greek text so took hold of me that for several days I could not stop reciting it to myself over and over.”[^corpus-weil-sweetness] “A week later I began the grape harvest. I recited the Our Father in Greek every day before work, and I repeated it very often in the vineyard.”[^corpus-weil-vineyard] “Since then I have made it my only practice to recite it once every morning with absolute attention.”[^corpus-weil-rule] If her mind drifts even slightly, she starts over, until she has said the whole prayer once without a moment’s distraction[^corpus-weil-again].

Her path is no model of church life, but her story points to something simple. If you have no topics of your own, there is the prayer Jesus gave His disciples as a pattern (Matthew 6:9–13). Weil wrote of it: “This prayer contains every possible petition; we cannot conceive of a prayer that is not already contained in it.”[^corpus-weil-all-requests] The main topics are already named there: God’s name and His will; our daily bread, meaning work, money and health; the forgiveness we ask for and the forgiveness we owe; the temptation and evil we ask to be delivered from. You can walk through it slowly and stop at whichever petition is about you today.

## When there are too many topics

In the sixth century two recluses, Barsanuphius and John, lived near Gaza[^barsanuphius]. Seridos sheltered them from the crowds[^corpus-barsanuphius-hidden]: he was abbot of the local monastery, and Barsanuphius sent his answers out through him[^corpus-barsanuphius-serid]. Their replies were collected into a book, known in English as their *Letters*, and the questions in it sound remarkably like ours.

Someone wrote to ask: when you are praying about many things, should you mention each one? The reply ran like this: since God knows what we need, you can simply ask the Lord to guide you as He wills. For your passions, ask Him to heal you according to His will; about temptations, say that He knows what is good for you and ask for help and deliverance[^corpus-barsanuphius-many]. Another asked how to pray for healing. John advised against straining to obtain it, since we do not know what is good for us. Better to tell God that you are in His hands, ask for mercy and, if it would do you good, a quick recovery, and to thank Him for everything[^corpus-barsanuphius-healing]. People even asked about money and property: how do you use what you own in a godly way when there is nobody to advise you? The answer is the same: admit that it all belongs to God, and ask Him to guide you and not let you go astray or misuse it[^corpus-barsanuphius-property].

This is the counterweight to M’Cheyne’s list, and both answers are honest. A list keeps you from forgetting. Trust keeps you from being afraid to forget.

## Things to pray for: a prayer list with questions

Here is a map drawn from these stories. You don’t have to go through all of it: pick one topic for today and ask yourself the question that goes with it.

**The people around you**

- Family: who among those closest to me is tired, ill or anxious right now?
- Coworkers, neighbors, chance encounters: who did I see today, and how are they doing?
- Someone who hurt me: what good can I wish them before God?
- People far away: whose trouble did I hear about today?

**The parts of prayer**

- Praise: what about God amazes me today?
- Thanksgiving: what am I grateful for, even something small?
- Confession: what did I get wrong, and what do I regret?
- Petition: what do I really want to ask for?
- Praying for others: who is waiting for my prayer?

**Through the Lord’s Prayer**

- God’s will: where is it hard for me to say “Your will be done”?
- Daily bread: what do I need today, whether work, money or strength?
- Forgiveness: whom do I need to forgive?
- Temptation: what is pulling me where I don’t want to go?

**When it’s all too much**

- One request: if I put it in a single sentence, what am I asking for?
- Health: can I ask for healing and leave the outcome to God?

If you have a lot of topics, keep a prayer list of your own, with names and needs. You can spread it across the week (M’Cheyne considered praying for different circles on different days[^corpus-mccheyne-days-list]) and read it over from time to time.

## Frequently asked questions {#faq}

### What should I ask God for?

Any real need, your own or someone else’s. And if you don’t know exactly what to ask, you can ask God to guide you as He wills, since He already knows what we need[^corpus-barsanuphius-many-faq].

### Can I pray about money, work and small things?

Yes. The apostle Paul says “in everything” (Philippians 4:6). The elders of Gaza were asked even what to say in prayer if you want to manage your property in a godly way and have no one to advise you[^corpus-barsanuphius-property-faq], and M’Cheyne wrote: “I am persuaded that I ought never to do anything without prayer”[^corpus-mccheyne-everything-faq].

### What are 5 things you should pray for?

There is no fixed rule. M’Cheyne named five parts of prayer: confession, adoration, thanksgiving, petition and intercession[^corpus-mccheyne-parts-faq]. He allowed that a single prayer need not include them all, but he was sure that each one deserved a place at some point every day[^corpus-mccheyne-each-day-faq]. Many Christians today use a similar pattern under the acronym ACTS: adoration, confession, thanksgiving, supplication.
