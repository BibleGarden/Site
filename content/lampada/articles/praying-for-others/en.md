Praying for someone means naming them before God and asking Him to help them. Sometimes that is easier said than done.

England, the middle of the nineteenth century. His mother was away from home, and her son had a free day. By his own later account he was about fifteen; that afternoon he went through his father’s books, looking for something to fill the empty hours[^corpus-taylor-holiday][^taylor-age]. Nothing appealed to him, so he pulled a gospel tract out of a basket and told himself: “There will be a story at the commencement, and a sermon or moral at the close: I will take the former and leave the latter for those who like it.”[^corpus-taylor-tract]

Meanwhile his mother was seventy or eighty miles away from him, and he had no idea what was happening in her heart[^corpus-taylor-miles]. That afternoon she got up from the table longing to pray for her son to come to faith[^corpus-taylor-table]. “She went to her room and turned the key in the door, resolved not to leave that spot until her prayers were answered.”[^corpus-taylor-door] She prayed for hours, until she could ask no longer and began to thank God instead: His Spirit, she believed, had shown her that her son was already converted[^corpus-taylor-praise].

When his mother returned two weeks later, her son was first at the door to meet her and tell her his good news[^corpus-taylor-meet]. She held him close: “I know, my boy; I have been rejoicing for a fortnight in the glad tidings you have to tell me.”[^corpus-taylor-know] He thought at once of his sister Amelia: a few days earlier he had worked up the courage to share his joy with her, but only after she promised to tell no one[^corpus-taylor-confidante]. Surprised, he asked whether Amelia had broken her word, but his mother said that no human being had told her[^corpus-taylor-amelia].

And that was not all. Some time later he opened a little notebook by mistake, taking it for his own, since the two looked exactly alike[^corpus-taylor-pocketbook]. It was his sister’s diary. She had written that “she would give herself daily to prayer until God should answer in the conversion of her brother”[^corpus-taylor-sister].

The boy’s name was Hudson Taylor. He grew up to be an evangelical missionary, founded the China Inland Mission and gave half a century of his life to China[^taylor]. Before he ever prayed for others, he found out that others had been praying for him, and he had never suspected a thing.

## What is intercessory prayer?

Praying for others, which Christians call intercessory prayer, means bringing to God not yourself but another person: their name, their situation and your worry for them. Starting is simple. Say the person’s name. Tell God honestly how things are for them right now and what worries you. Ask God to give them what they need, whether health, peace, reconciliation, protection or faith, and don’t dictate to Him how it should happen.

Our article on [what to pray about](/articles/what-to-pray-about/) treats praying for others as one topic among several. This one is about how to pray for a particular person, especially when it is hard: when they turn away, when they hurt you, or when they have dropped out of sight.

## The man who turned away

Shortly before he left for China, Taylor had to dress, every day, the foot of an old man who was dying of senile gangrene. The Christian family the man lived with told Taylor that he openly called himself an atheist and was hostile to anything to do with faith[^corpus-taylor-atheist]. When they brought in a Scripture reader without asking him, he flew into a rage and drove the man out, and when the local vicar called, he spat in his face[^corpus-taylor-vicar].

Taylor prayed a great deal for him, but for the first two or three days said nothing about faith and simply looked after him, taking care to ease his pain. When the patient began to thank him, Taylor, his heart trembling, spoke to him of his need for God’s mercy[^corpus-taylor-first-days]. “He turned over in bed with his back to me, and uttered no word.”[^corpus-taylor-back] It went on like that day after day: many times a day Taylor begged God to save the man, after each dressing he said a few words, and every time the man turned away from him in silence[^corpus-taylor-daily]. In the end Taylor lost heart: he began to fear that his visits were useless and were perhaps only hardening the man further[^corpus-taylor-sank]. One day, after dressing the wound and washing his hands, he did not go back to the bed but stopped at the door, thinking: “Ephraim is joined to his idols; let him alone.”[^corpus-taylor-ephraim]

The patient looked at him in surprise: for the first time Taylor was about to leave without a word. And Taylor could not bear it. In tears, he crossed the room and pleaded with the man to let him pray with him[^corpus-taylor-tears]. The old man did not turn away: “If it will be a relief to you, do.”[^corpus-taylor-relief] Within days, Taylor tells us, the man accepted Christ[^corpus-taylor-accepted].

Taylor’s conclusion is not about the patient but about himself. Sometimes, he wondered, the real obstacle lies with those who are trying to help: their own hard hearts, and how little they grasp the reality of eternal things[^corpus-taylor-hardness]. Notice something else, too. The prayer went along with the bandages, not instead of them. And Taylor prayed with the man only once the man agreed.

## When you’d rather not pray for them

All of Russia knew John of Kronstadt, an Orthodox priest: crowds of thousands came out to meet him, and people revered him as a man of prayer[^ioann]. Yet the diary he kept for himself shows an ordinary man whose feelings had been hurt: “I hear that the elder of the Gethsemane Skete near the Sergius Lavra, Father Barnabas, has spoken unkindly of me. What have I done to him? I must pray that the Lord will reconcile our hearts”[^corpus-ioann-varnava][^varnava].

He does not ask God to set Barnabas straight. He asks Him to reconcile their hearts, both of them, his own included. The diary does not say how it ended.

Right after that comes another entry. The wife of a man who had once been his secretary came to see him and was rude to him, after all the help he had given their family[^corpus-ioann-secretary]. “I confess I could not stand it and sharply asked her to leave my house”[^corpus-ioann-door].

Jesus said, “love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you” (Matthew 5:44)[^mt5]. John’s diary shows what that looks like in a real person’s life: praying for someone who hurt you starts with being honest about the hurt and asking God to change your own heart. And one entry does not settle it.

## When the person is far away

People traveled to Optina Monastery to ask Elder Ambrose for advice, and those who could not come wrote to him; Dostoevsky took him as a model for the elder Zosima[^ambrose]. One day a letter came to him from a mother whose son had abandoned his faith. She blamed herself for not bringing him up as she should have, and she admitted that in her grief she had sometimes thought he would be better off dead than living as he was[^corpus-ambrose-guilt]. She asked whether she should write to him in Moscow on the off chance it would reach him, and how to word the letter so that it would touch his heart[^corpus-ambrose-write].

The elder was late in replying: he had been ill for more than a month and wrote that he was still very weak[^corpus-ambrose-ill]. The letter is dated November 22, 1869[^corpus-ambrose-date]. For having thought her son would be better off dead, he told her to reproach herself and to entrust both herself and her son to God[^corpus-ambrose-will]. But he did not want her to take all the blame either: “every person is endowed with free will and will have to answer before God above all for himself”[^corpus-ambrose-freewill]. As for her main question, how to write to him, the elder answered: “But first, I repeat, you need to find out where he is at present and what his circumstances are”[^corpus-ambrose-where]. And then: “you should now be concerned not so much with bringing him to his senses as with praying for him, so that the Lord Himself, by ways known to Him, may bring him to his senses”[^corpus-ambrose-pray]. And one more thing: “And while you pray for your son, pray for yourself as well”[^corpus-ambrose-self].

We don’t know how the story ended. What remains is the advice: less of your own script, more trust.

That is how Jesus prayed for Peter on the eve of his denial: “But I have prayed for you, Simon, that your faith will not fail. And when you have turned back, strengthen your brothers” (Luke 22:32)[^lk22]. Knowing that Peter would deny Him, Jesus prays for Peter’s faith and for his return.

## Prayer and action

Elizabeth Wheaton was known as “Mother Wheaton”; for more than twenty years she traveled from prison to prison with the gospel[^wheaton]. In 1898, in a city jail, she met a young man under sentence of death, and his case stayed with her. When she looked into it, she learned that he had not even been charged with murder and did not deserve to die[^corpus-wheaton-boy]. “I plead to God if there was nothing the law could find in him worthy of death, that his sentence might be commuted, and the poor boy might live.”[^corpus-wheaton-plead] She did not stop at prayer. Back from her travels, she went to see the governor in the state capital and asked him to change the boy’s sentence to life in prison[^corpus-wheaton-governor]. She got what she asked for, and the boy was allowed to live[^corpus-wheaton-granted].

In 1899 she visited two condemned men the day before their execution. She talked with them and sang their favorite hymns with them, and then, as she remembered it:

> we three kneeled together in prayer in the little “condemned cell.”[^corpus-wheaton-kneel] Kneeling between my boys, clasping each by the hand, we gave ourselves to the blessed Savior…[^corpus-wheaton-hands]

The two men sang and prayed all night, and in the morning they “went to the scaffold singing and praising God”[^corpus-wheaton-singing]. This time her story has no governor and no reprieve.

For her, prayer and action went together. But prayer is not a lever that can move any decision you like. Sometimes the whole answer is that on their last night a person is not alone.

## How to pray for someone

If you don’t know how the person is doing, find out first; that is where Ambrose began. Ask for what is good for them, not for your own plan. And if you have no idea what to ask for, the words of the American preacher S. D. Gordon may help. In *Quiet Talks on Prayer* he admits that he often does not know all the facts about a person[^corpus-gordon-facts], and then he prays like this: “Blessed Spirit, pray in me the prayer Thou art praying for this man”[^corpus-gordon-spirit].

Put yourself into the prayer too: “reconcile our hearts.” For someone who hurt you, start with one honest sentence. If you lose your temper with them again tomorrow, so did John of Kronstadt. But praying for someone who hurt you does not cancel out boundaries, protection or your right to ask for help: from the people close to you, a pastor or priest, a professional, and, if you are being threatened, the police.

If you can help in practical ways, help. Offer to pray together, and wait until the person says yes. And jot down, briefly, whom you are praying for and what you are asking, so you can come back to it.

## Frequently asked questions {#faq}

### What is intercessory prayer?

It is prayer for another person: you ask God not for yourself but for them. The Gospels give an example: Jesus prayed for Peter that his faith would not fail (Luke 22:32)[^lk22].

### Can I pray for someone who hurt me?

Yes. Jesus tells us plainly to pray for those who persecute us (Matthew 5:44)[^mt5]. When he was hurt, John of Kronstadt prayed “that the Lord will reconcile our hearts”[^corpus-ioann-varnava-faq]. But prayer does not cancel out boundaries or protection.

### Should I tell someone I’m praying for them?

You don’t have to. Taylor learned of his sister’s prayers only when he opened someone else’s notebook by mistake, thinking it was his own[^corpus-taylor-pocketbook-faq]. But when Taylor wanted to pray with the sick man, he asked his permission, and the man agreed[^corpus-taylor-relief-faq].
