Newly ordained, a priest began serving in a home for older people. After his first service, a 101-year-old woman asked his advice[^corpus-woman-age]: “For many, many years I have been saying the Jesus Prayer constantly, and I have never once felt the presence of God.”[^corpus-woman-never] She had exhausted every expert. Perhaps this novice, who knew so little, might say something useful by accident[^corpus-woman-blurt].

So he said to her: “What do you think: when is God supposed to get a word in, or make His presence felt, if you are talking all the time?”[^corpus-woman-talk] The advice that followed was strange. First she was to tidy her room, then sit facing the icons and their burning lamp, the open window and photographs of loved ones[^corpus-woman-room]. Then she was to take up her needles and wool: “knit in silence before the face of God, and don’t you dare say a single prayer; just sit quietly and knit.”[^corpus-woman-knit]

That priest was the future Metropolitan Anthony of Sourozh, a doctor who later spent many years as a bishop in London[^antony]. He tells the story himself in one of his books, in a chapter on prayer. We’ll come back to how it ended.

Quiet time with God is time alone with Him in which you don’t only talk but also fall silent. It usually includes a little Scripture, prayer in your own words and some silence. In that silence, “hearing God” most often doesn’t mean hearing a voice. It means noticing that He is near, remembering a word of Scripture, seeing the truth about yourself. Christians test anything that seems to come “from God” against Scripture, with people they trust and over time. And if nothing happens in the silence, you haven’t failed.

## A voice that said what was already written

England, the 1640s. George Fox, a former shoemaker’s apprentice, left home at nineteen to look for God[^fox]. He fasted, wandered alone and sat with his Bible in hollow trees until dark[^corpus-fox-trees]. Neither clergy nor dissenting preachers nor experienced believers could help him, so he turned away from them all[^corpus-fox-none]. And when there was nothing left to hope for from people, he heard a voice: “There is one, even Christ Jesus, that can speak to thy condition”[^corpus-fox-voice]; “and when I heard it, my heart did leap for joy.”[^corpus-fox-joy]

Later, when Fox was already preaching, he was sitting by the fire one morning. Temptation settled over him like a heavy cloud, with a thought that kept pressing in: everything simply happens of itself, by nature[^corpus-fox-cloud]. Outwardly nothing showed; he stayed silent, and the household had no idea what he was going through[^corpus-fox-still]. Then came the turn he describes:

> And as I sat still under it and let it alone, a living hope and a true voice arose in me, which said, “There is a living God who made all things.”[^corpus-fox-living]

At once, he writes, “the cloud and temptation vanished away.”[^corpus-fox-vanished]

Notice what came to him in the silence. Not a secret revelation, but the first line of the Bible and the heart of the gospel. Fox, one of the founders of the Quakers, trusted the inner light more than many of his contemporaries did. Once he heard a preacher tell a congregation that every teaching was to be tried by Scripture. Fox felt the power of the Lord come over him so strongly that he could not keep quiet and cried out[^corpus-fox-steeple]: “Oh, no; it is not the Scriptures!”[^corpus-fox-cry] What tests teachings, he told them, is the Holy Spirit, the same Spirit who inspired the writers of Scripture[^corpus-fox-spirit]. This was in Nottingham, where Fox was put in prison for the first time[^fox]. People argued with him then, and they still do. But for him, too, silence led back to what matters most, not off to the side.

## Ten years of getting it wrong

In a 1915 book whose title translates as *Citizens of Heaven*, the writer and future priest Valentin Sventsitsky recounts visiting hermits in the Caucasus[^svenc]. One night in a forest clearing, old Father Nikifor told his story. Once a shop assistant and then a wealthy owner, he had, by his own account, lived badly. After his wife died, he parted with his money, spent years at New Athos and became a hermit[^corpus-nikifor-life].

And there he nearly lost his mind. “I prayed the wrong way. For ten years I went on like that, getting it wrong.”[^corpus-nikifor-ten] In prayer he pictured Christ in the sky and bowed before the image. He later understood this as a demon’s deception. Whenever his mind tired, another vision appeared, a whole host of angels, which he also put down to the demon, and curiosity drew him back[^corpus-nikifor-images]. He had been warned: “The brethren told me: you mustn’t pray like that. Those words pierced my heart like a spear; I’d get angry and walk away. And again I’d pray my own way.”[^corpus-nikifor-brethren] In the end he told one of the monks, “Today or tomorrow I’ll go out of my mind.”[^corpus-nikifor-mad] The “demon” is Nikifor’s own explanation, not a diagnosis.

In silence, God is not the only one who speaks. Our imagination speaks too, and so do our wishes and fears. Nikifor’s trouble wasn’t that he made mistakes: everyone does. It was that for ten years he wouldn’t let anyone correct him.

## How to test what seems to be “from God”

The apostle John writes: “Beloved, do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits to see whether they are from God. For many false prophets have gone out into the world” (1 John 4:1)[^1jn]. Both stories show how to do it. The first test is Scripture. What Fox heard agrees with the first line of the Bible. If a “voice” contradicts the gospel, it isn’t from God. The second test is the people around you. Nikifor’s brothers tried to correct him, and he should have listened.

The third test is time. F. B. Meyer, an English Baptist pastor and a friend of the evangelist D. L. Moody[^meyer], wrote in *The Secret of Guidance*: “God’s impressions within and His word without are always corroborated by His Providence around, and we should quietly wait until these three focus into one point.”[^corpus-meyer-three] Or more briefly: “If you do not know what you ought to do, stand still until you do.”[^corpus-meyer-still] He himself became sure of God’s guidance “after months of waiting and prayer.”[^corpus-meyer-months]

Be wary if a “voice” rushes you, flatters you, frightens you, tells you to keep it secret from everyone or argues with Scripture. And if intrusive voices or images won’t leave you alone, there is nothing shameful about seeing a doctor.

## Contemplative, centering and breath prayer

A week later Anthony was serving in the same home again and, he admits, looked around hoping the old woman wasn’t there[^corpus-woman-hope]. She came: “Father Anthony, you know what — it works!”[^corpus-woman-works] The click of her needles, once she began to notice it, made the quiet around her feel deeper still[^corpus-woman-needles]. Then she sensed that the silence was “not simply an absence of noise, but that there was something else in it, that at the very heart of this silence there was some kind of presence.”[^corpus-woman-presence] And she realized: “I can pray with words or not pray, it’s all the same: I am with Him.”[^corpus-woman-with]

This is **contemplative prayer**: not thinking about God, but simply being in His presence. Anthony also told of an old man a priest often saw in church: he would sit there, looking ahead of him, saying nothing[^corpus-peasant-sit]. Asked what he was doing there for hours, the old man answered: “I look at Him, He looks at me, and we are so happy with each other.”[^corpus-peasant] But Anthony immediately added a caveat: we cannot live like this all the time, and prayers in words are necessary, because we don’t reach that silence all at once on our own[^corpus-antony-words].

**Centering prayer** is a method put forward in the 1970s by American Trappist monks[^centering]. Its root is *The Cloud of Unknowing*, an English book from the fourteenth century by an unknown author[^cloud]. He advises picking a short word of one syllable, such as “God” or “love,” and holding it fast in the heart[^corpus-cloud-word]. “This word shall be thy shield and thy spear”[^corpus-cloud-shield]: it is how you beat back every thought that pulls you away[^corpus-cloud-thought].

**Breath prayer** is a short prayer said in the rhythm of your breathing. In the Orthodox tradition, the Jesus Prayer is sometimes joined to the breath. The hero of *The Way of a Pilgrim*, a book first published in Kazan in 1884[^pilgrim], heard the reading “Pray without ceasing” during the liturgy, and the words would not leave him: how could anyone pray without stopping and still work for a living?[^corpus-pilgrim-start] An elder went through the Philokalia with him and explained how to join the Jesus Prayer to the breath[^corpus-pilgrim-breath]. But breathing is not a key that unlocks God. A hermit on Mount Athos, in a conversation recorded by Metropolitan Hierotheos (Vlachos), allows breathing techniques as an aid to attention but insists on supervision and an elder’s special blessing[^corpus-hierotheos]. Remember the old woman: she had repeated the Jesus Prayer for years, and she sensed God when she fell silent.

What sets all this apart from secular meditation is the goal. The point is not to empty your head or to relax, though peace may come. We are silent before Someone.

## How to try it today

Clear away distractions, put your phone aside and sit somewhere quiet. Read a few Scripture verses; see [lectio divina](/articles/lectio-divina/) and [Praying Scripture](/articles/praying-scripture/). Tell God [in your own words](/articles/how-to-start-praying/) what is on your mind. Then fall silent without demanding feelings or answers. When distracted, return with a short word or the Jesus Prayer. Breathe naturally, without exercises. Close with thanks, a request or the Lord’s Prayer. Take whatever time you have.

If a thought comes in the silence that seems to be “from God,” don’t rush. Check it against Scripture, tell someone you trust and wait.

## Frequently asked questions {#faq}

### How can I “hear” God?

Most often not as a voice, but as His presence, a word of Scripture or the truth about yourself. Whatever seems to come “from God” is tested by Scripture, other people and time: “do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits to see whether they are from God” (1 John 4:1)[^1jn-faq].

### What are centering prayer and breath prayer?

Centering prayer is silence with one short “sacred word” that you return to when your attention wanders; in its present form it was put forward in the 1970s by American Trappist monks drawing on the medieval *Cloud of Unknowing*[^centering]. Breath prayer is a short prayer in the rhythm of your breathing: this is how, for example, the Jesus Prayer is joined to the breath[^corpus-pilgrim-breath-faq].

### What if nothing happens in the silence?

A lack of special feelings doesn’t make your prayer a failure. You can close it with words and go back to your day: even Metropolitan Anthony of Sourozh cautioned that we cannot live in silent prayer all the time and that we need prayers in words[^corpus-antony-words-faq].
