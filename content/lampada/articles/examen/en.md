England, around 1770. A teenage girl in the small town of Macclesfield read in a devout book that at the judgment everyone’s deeds would be weighed: if the good outweighed the bad, the way to heaven lay open, and if the bad outweighed the good, condemnation awaited; she decided to test herself honestly by this rule[^corpus-rogers-rule]. “I therefore made a little day-book, in which I put down every good and bad action”[^corpus-rogers-daybook]. One day at a dance she pulled her handkerchief out of her pocket, and the day-book came out with it: “it was found, and made the jest of the company”[^corpus-rogers-dance]. Mortified, she abandoned her method[^corpus-rogers-quit]. The girl was Hester Ann Roe; later, as Hester Rogers, she would become a well-known Methodist writer[^rogers].

**The examen** (from the Latin for the tongue of a balance, and so a weighing or a test)[^examen-word] is a short prayer in which you look back over your day together with God. Ignatius of Loyola described it in his *Spiritual Exercises*. You will also hear it called the daily examen, the examination of conscience or, in today’s Ignatian spirituality, the examen of consciousness[^examination]. A few minutes is enough: Sacred Space, the prayer site of the Irish Jesuits, suggests five or ten[^sacred-space]. It isn’t confession: Catholics also examine their conscience before the sacrament of reconciliation, but the examen is a daily personal prayer[^examination]. Nor is it a journal: you don’t have to write anything down.

In Ignatius the examen has five movements: give thanks, ask for light, go back over the day, ask forgiveness, and resolve, with God’s grace, to do better. It isn’t a mandatory template, and it isn’t bookkeeping like Hester’s day-book. You can shorten the steps; what matters is that you are not looking at your day alone.

## The wounded soldier who listened to his thoughts

In May 1521, during the defense of Pamplona, a cannonball shattered the leg of a Basque nobleman named Íñigo de Loyola. His recovery was long. There were no tales of chivalry in the castle, so he was given a life of Christ and lives of the saints instead. He noticed that daydreams of glory left him empty, while thinking about the saints left him joyful and at peace. Later, with six companions, he founded the Jesuits[^ignatius]. The Society of Jesus became the largest Catholic religious order of men, known for its schools and universities and, from the sixteenth century on, for missions all over the world[^jesuits]. Ignatius made the examen obligatory for his brothers twice a day, at midday and in the evening, and Jesuits still pray it today[^sacred-space-jesuits].

In the *Spiritual Exercises* the general examen of conscience does not start with sins. Here are its five points in Elder Mullan’s English translation:

- “The first Point is to give thanks to God our Lord for the benefits received.”[^corpus-ignatius-thanks]
- The second: “to ask grace to know our sins and cast them out.”[^corpus-ignatius-grace]
- The third: “to ask account of our soul from the hour that we rose up to the present Examen, hour by hour, or period by period”[^corpus-ignatius-account], in thoughts, words and deeds.
- The fourth: “to ask pardon of God our Lord for the faults.”[^corpus-ignatius-pardon]
- The fifth: “to purpose amendment with His grace.”[^corpus-ignatius-amend]

Then the Our Father. Notice that light is something you ask God for, not something you wring out of yourself by self-reproach. The psalmist prayed the same way: “Search me, O God, and know my heart; test me and know my concerns. See if there is any offensive way in me; lead me in the way everlasting” (Psalm 139:23–24)[^ps139].

Ignatius could count, too. In the “particular examen”, an exercise aimed at one chosen fault, you go back over the hours once more after supper and put a dot on a line for every lapse into that fault[^corpus-ignatius-dots]. But even this counting sits inside a prayer: after the midday meal you ask God for the grace to remember how often you have fallen into that fault and to amend, and once you have counted, you resolve afresh to do better until the next examen[^corpus-ignatius-particular]. And the general examen begins with gratitude. Hester was hoping for something else: to tell from the balance of her good deeds whether there was any hope for her soul. Obedience to her elders, prayers and church services all counted. To add one more good deed she would sometimes skip games and parties and stay home reading to her mother, and now and then she fasted for whole days, yet the bad still outweighed the good[^corpus-rogers-swell].

## Abba Dorotheus’s questions

Long before Ignatius, in the sixth century, Abba Dorotheus lived in a monastery near Gaza. His teachings shaped monks in both East and West[^dorotheus]. He passes on a rule the fathers left: every evening, look back on how you spent the day, and every morning on how you spent the night, and repent before God of whatever you find[^corpus-dorotheus-evening]. His questions are strikingly down to earth: did I hurt my brother, did I judge him when I saw him at his work, did I grumble against the cellarer who refused me, did I complain about bad food[^corpus-dorotheus-questions].

He doesn’t spare himself. Not long after a serious illness he was put in charge of receiving guests at the monastery: he spent the evenings with travelers, then served the camel drivers, and at night he was woken again[^corpus-dorotheus-guests]. The monk who woke the brothers for the night vigil would come to rouse him, and Dorotheus, exhausted, would mumble in his sleep that he was coming, then drift off again and later grieve over being late. His solution was simple: he asked one brother to wake him and another to keep him from dozing at the vigil. He honored these two as men through whom God was bringing about his salvation[^corpus-dorotheus-brothers]. His look back ended not in beating himself up but in asking for help.

Dorotheus promises slow change: whoever examines himself like this, repents and tries to amend will commit eight faults instead of nine, little by little, with God’s help[^corpus-dorotheus-nine].

## “My poor heart”

What if, at the fourth step, something bad comes to mind? Francis de Sales, bishop of Geneva and author of the *Introduction to the Devout Life*[^sales], advised against scolding yourself. A French priest, Joseph Tissot, gathered passages from his letters and books into a short volume on how to make good use of our faults. De Sales warns against harshly berating your own heart[^corpus-sales-shame]. Instead, speak like this: “Well, my poor heart, here we are, fallen into the very pit we had so firmly resolved to escape. Ah! let us get up and leave it for good”[^corpus-sales-heart]. To a woman who complained that she still took slights painfully, he replied: “But, my dear daughter, what does this ‘still’ refer to?”[^corpus-sales-encore] And he reminded her: “we are little chicks that don’t yet have our wings”[^corpus-sales-chicks].

## John Woolman’s night

Looking back in the evening is not only about the past. John Woolman was a Quaker and a tailor from New Jersey, one of the first to speak out against slavery[^woolman]. In the spring of 1772 he was looking for a passage to England. As he tells it in his *Journal*, his friend had taken a place in the cabin of a ship bound for London, while Woolman felt drawn toward the steerage of the same ship[^corpus-woolman-draught], the cramped common quarters below deck. They looked over the cabin, but sat down below on a chest among the sailors. There, his mind turned toward Christ, Woolman felt his own will yield, and though the owner invited them up to the cabin, he left the ship with a light heart without taking a place on her[^corpus-woolman-leave].

That night, he recalls, “my Mind was under a deep Exercise before the Lord; whose helping Hand was manifested to me as I slept that Night, and his Love strengthened my Heart”[^corpus-woolman-night]. In the morning he went to the owner and told him of the scruple of conscience that kept him from traveling in the cabin[^corpus-woolman-owner]. Only then did he arrange to travel below deck[^corpus-woolman-agreed]. A week later, at sea, he wrote: “I have, this Day, great Cause to be thankful, in that I remain to feel Quietness of Mind”[^corpus-woolman-quiet].

This isn’t the examen, and it isn’t a review of a whole day: Woolman is describing a night of wrestling before one particular decision. But the movement is similar: notice where your soul has no peace, bring it to God, and take a step in the morning. Why the cabin troubled him, Woolman doesn’t say in these lines. That autumn he fell ill with smallpox in England and died in York[^woolman-death].

## How to pray the examen tonight

This evening, even for five minutes, recall one specific thing you want to thank God for. Ask: “Show me this day.” Walk through it person by person, moment by moment: whom did I hurt or judge, as Dorotheus asked, and where was there peace and where heaviness, as with Woolman. Talk to yourself about what went wrong the way de Sales did, not like a prosecutor, and ask for forgiveness. Choose one small step for tomorrow; sometimes it will be asking for help, as Dorotheus did. Finish with the Lord’s Prayer or in your own words.

You don’t have to write anything down. If you’d like to, one line is enough; for how to keep notes and read them back, see our article on [keeping a prayer journal](/articles/prayer-journal/). If you already pray in the evening from a prayer book or with the Daily Office, the examen can sit alongside those prayers rather than replace them; on finding a regular rhythm of prayer, see our article on the [rule of prayer](/articles/prayer-rule/). There are two traps: turning the evening into bookkeeping, as Hester did, or into a sleepless interrogation of yourself. If looking back over the day keeps leaving you anxious, cut it down to gratitude and trust, and talk to a priest or pastor. If the anxiety won’t let go of you, see a mental health professional.

Hester did not find her way out in a new day-book. ## Frequently asked questions {#faq}

### What is the examen prayer?

A short prayer in which you look back over your day with God. In Ignatius of Loyola’s words, its first point is “to give thanks to God our Lord for the benefits received.”[^corpus-ignatius-thanks-faq] Then come asking for light, going back over the day, asking forgiveness and resolving, with God’s grace, to do better.

### How long does the examen take?

A few minutes. Sacred Space, the Irish Jesuits’ prayer site, speaks of five to ten[^sacred-space]. Ignatius asked the Jesuits to pray it twice a day, at midday and at the end of the day[^sacred-space-jesuits].

### Can non-Catholics pray the examen?

The name and the five points come from the Jesuits, but other Christians also know the practice of looking back on the day in the evening. Abba Dorotheus passed on the rule of the fathers: every evening, look back on how you spent the day[^corpus-dorotheus-evening-faq].

### What if all I remember in the evening is my mistakes?

Start with gratitude and speak to yourself gently. Francis de Sales advised addressing yourself like this: “Well, my poor heart, here we are, fallen into the pit”[^corpus-sales-heart-faq]. If the evening still turns into an anxious interrogation, talk to a priest, a pastor or a mental health professional.

### Is there a guided examen in Lampada?

Not a ready-made examen with five steps. You can set your intention as “Look back on today” and pray with guiding questions that you can skip; at the end the app asks a closing question about this prayer.
