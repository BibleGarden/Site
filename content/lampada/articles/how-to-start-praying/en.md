Silvio Pellico was a celebrated Italian playwright, and the Austrian police suspected him of belonging to the Carbonari, a secret revolutionary society[^pellico]. On 13 October 1820 he was arrested in Milan and locked up in the Santa Margherita prison[^corpus-pellico-arrest]. Once the interrogations were over, loneliness came down on him with its full weight, and for reading he had only a Bible, Dante and a few novels from the jailer’s own shelf[^corpus-pellico-cell]. It was in that cell that Pellico learned to pray the way you may be hoping to learn: in his own words.

So how do you start praying? Talk to God about what is on your heart right now, in whatever words you have. You don’t need polished phrases or a “proper” formula, and written prayers are a good support on the days when your own words won’t come. The people who found their way into prayer like this could hardly be more different: an Italian Catholic prisoner, a Canadian Presbyterian missionary, a French Carmelite nun and a Russian Orthodox bishop who spent his last two decades as a recluse. Here are their stories.

## “Am I not in excellent company?”

Pellico had loved the Bible deeply even in the years when he thought of himself as an unbeliever, and now, when nothing else he read could hold his attention, a passage from it sometimes still did[^corpus-pellico-bible]. One day he set himself a goal, that every thought he had would be moved by the wish to bend to God’s will. What prompted it was something he had read, that we must pray without ceasing, and that true prayer is not mumbling a great many words, as the pagans do, but worshipping God simply, in word and in deed[^corpus-pellico-pagans]. What he had read echoes Jesus’ words in the Sermon on the Mount: “And when you pray, do not babble on like pagans, for they think that by their many words they will be heard. Do not be like them, for your Father knows what you need before you ask Him” (Matthew 6:7–8)[^mt6].

Pellico used few set prayers. Not out of contempt, since he believed they were very helpful. He simply knew he could not recite many of them without his mind wandering off until he forgot he was worshipping at all[^corpus-pellico-formulas]. Instead, he tried to remember all day long that God was near, and with every passing day his solitude frightened him less[^corpus-pellico-presence]. “Am I not in excellent company?”[^corpus-pellico-company] he would tell himself, and he grew calm and even started humming and whistling[^corpus-pellico-whistle].

## A little girl with a toothache

Rosalind Goforth, a Presbyterian missionary, spent nearly half a century in China. In the summer of 1900 she and her husband fled across hundreds of miles of the country to escape the Boxer uprising, and of her eleven children, five died young[^goforth]. In 1921 she published *How I Know God Answers Prayer*, the testimony of a lifetime. Telling the story of her life, she begins with her childhood.

From the time she was tiny, whenever something went wrong, Rosalind’s habit was to take it straight to Jesus[^corpus-goforth-tell]. She was six or seven and playing in the garden when a tooth suddenly began to ache badly. Her mother tried to comfort her, but nothing helped[^corpus-goforth-pain]. So, with her face hidden against her mother, the little girl prayed without a sound: “Lord Jesus, if you will take away this toothache right now, now, I will be your little girl for three years.”[^corpus-goforth-prayer]

Before the words were even out, the pain had vanished, and she was certain Jesus had taken it. The child took the deal seriously. For years, any time mischief tempted her, fear held her back, because breaking her promise might bring the toothache back[^corpus-goforth-deal]. A child bargaining with God can look naive, but what Goforth herself treasured about that day was something else: it left her with an abiding awareness that God was truly present[^corpus-goforth-presence].

This is her testimony, not a promise that every pain will vanish after a prayer. But there is something in it for anyone: a small girl started with what hurt right then, and she didn’t go looking for the right words first.

## “Like children who cannot read”

Thérèse Martin entered the Carmelite convent in Lisieux at fifteen and died there of tuberculosis at twenty-four. Her notebooks, published after her death as *The Story of a Soul*, became one of the most widely read spiritual books of the twentieth century, and the Catholic Church declared her a saint.

Those notebooks contain a disarmingly honest admission. With so many beautiful prayers to choose from, picking one out of a book gives her a headache, so she does not go hunting for them, even though she gladly recites the Church’s daily Office[^corpus-therese-books]. Instead, she writes: “I do as children do who cannot read. I simply tell God what I want to say to Him, and He always understands me.”[^corpus-therese-children]

That doesn’t mean she throws written prayers away. On dry days her lifeline is the Our Father or the Hail Mary. When her mind is so parched that not one good thought will surface, she prays one of them as unhurriedly as she can, and she needs nothing more: that prayer by itself fills her with joy and nourishes her soul[^corpus-therese-pater]. For Thérèse, her own words and the Church’s words are not rivals: when one fails her, the other carries her.

## A prayer book is a phrasebook

Theophan the Recluse was a Russian Orthodox bishop. At fifty-one he gave up his diocese and settled at the Vysha Hermitage, and he spent the last twenty-two years of his life there in seclusion, from 1872 until his death in 1894. The mail brought him twenty to forty letters a day, and he answered every one[^feofan].

His *Letters on Prayer* include a reply to someone who confessed, clearly worried: “I no longer pray as well using prayer books”[^corpus-feofan-worry]. Theophan answered: “That is no loss”[^corpus-feofan-no-harm]. Then he explained with an unexpected comparison: “Prayer books are just like phrasebooks, French ones for example”[^corpus-feofan-phrasebook]. In other words, ready-made texts are something to lean on while you learn. Once the soul can turn to God by itself, the book can be set aside[^corpus-feofan-until].

It matters who this was written to: someone already used to praying from a prayer book. Theophan tells him he may pray without the book whenever prayer is alive in his heart, and in the same breath warns him not to let laziness hide behind it[^corpus-feofan-sloth]. And when his own prayer will not come, Theophan advises starting with printed prayers to stir it up, and knowing a few prayers and psalms by heart[^corpus-feofan-printed].

Our own advice, not Theophan’s, for anyone who prays with a prayer rule or a prayer book, whether Orthodox, Catholic or Anglican: your own words don’t replace those prayers, they come alongside them.

## When prayer won’t come

Pellico’s prayer wasn’t steady either. Nightmares began to torment him. One night he woke with the feeling that he was being strangled. His own handkerchief was wound tightly around his neck and tied in several knots, and he had no memory of doing it[^corpus-pellico-terror]. By day he joked with his fellow prisoners and his jailers, but at sunset he would start to shiver, and in the end he was gripped by the dreadful thought that God had abandoned him for good[^corpus-pellico-abandoned]. Doubt followed. He held out for days against the clever-sounding case against Providence, but in the end he let it win, and he began to talk like the furious atheists he had heard, insisting that religion does nothing but weaken the mind. He later wondered whether an illness explains it all, or whether God was also using it to bring his pride low[^corpus-pellico-doubt]. When it passed, overflowing with gratitude, he threw himself on his knees and begged God to forgive him for denying Him those days, and he fell asleep right there, still kneeling, leaning against a chair[^corpus-pellico-return].

The hardest part was still ahead. When he learned his fate, the first thing Pellico did was sit down and write to his father. He wanted his family to hear the terrible news from him rather than from anyone else[^corpus-pellico-letter]. That night he could not sleep for thinking of his elderly parents. Would they survive such a blow?[^corpus-pellico-night] He thought, too, about the judge, whom he imagined gloating[^corpus-pellico-judge]. The night was hell: he could not forgive, nor did he want to; hatred tasted sweeter[^corpus-pellico-hate]. He admits it plainly: “In the morning, I did not pray.”[^corpus-pellico-no-prayer] And he goes on: “I felt wretched that I could not pray; but where pride reigns, one finds no other God than oneself.”[^corpus-pellico-pride]

Then he was led out onto a platform in the middle of the square by the Doge’s Palace, in front of a huge crowd[^corpus-pellico-scaffold]. First came the death sentence, and a murmur of pity ran through the crowd. Then the reading went on, and for Pellico himself it came to fifteen years of harsh imprisonment[^corpus-pellico-sentence].

Pellico doesn’t hide that night or turn it into a tidy moral. But his honesty suggests a useful question: if prayer suddenly won’t come, what am I hiding from God right now? Resentment, anger and pride can be named out loud, and that, too, is praying in your own words.

And if you have no words at all, the apostle Paul offers comfort: “In the same way, the Spirit helps us in our weakness. For we do not know how we ought to pray, but the Spirit Himself intercedes for us with groans too deep for words” (Romans 8:26)[^rom8].

## How to start praying today

- **Start with what is on your heart, or what hurts.** One sentence is enough, as it was for little Rosalind.
- **Use ordinary words.** Thérèse didn’t hunt for beautiful prayers, and she was sure God understood her.
- **When words won’t come, lean on written prayers.** Read the Lord’s Prayer or a psalm very slowly, then carry on in your own words. Theophan advised this, and Thérèse did it.
- **Name whatever is in the way.** Resentment and pride took Pellico’s prayer from him; telling God about them is already the start of a conversation.
- **Don’t wait for the right place or time.** Pellico prayed in a prison cell, Rosalind with her face hidden against her mother.

## Frequently asked questions {#faq}

### Do I need to know prayers by heart to start?

No, you can talk to God in your own words. But a few prayers and psalms you know by heart will help on the days when your own words are gone, as Theophan the Recluse advised above.

### How do I start and end a prayer?

There is no required formula. You can simply address God (“Father”, “Lord” or “Lord Jesus”, as Rosalind did) and then say what is on your heart. Many Christians close with “Amen”, which means “so be it”, or with “in Jesus’ name, Amen”.

### Can I pray silently, not out loud?

Yes. Little Rosalind Goforth prayed about her toothache silently, without saying a word out loud[^corpus-goforth-heart].

### What if prayer feels empty or boring?

Don’t give up and don’t blame yourself. You can start with a written prayer, read very slowly, and return to your own words when they come back. That is what Thérèse of Lisieux did.
