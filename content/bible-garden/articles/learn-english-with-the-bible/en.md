The Bible works well for learning English because many learners already know its stories. You know what happens, so you can focus on how it is said. The method takes three steps per chapter:

1. **Understand:** each verse plays twice, in two English wordings or at two speeds.
2. **Listen:** play the same chapter once in English, following the text with your eyes.
3. **Repeat:** say a few verses aloud after the narrator.

Start with the Gospel of John in the Berean Standard Bible (BSB), a modern translation. You can do all of this in the free [Bible Garden app](/) for iPhone and iPad. Below: how to set up each step, which English translation is easier, a 30-day plan, and famous Bible verses with their best-known idioms.

## Why the Bible works for learning English
<!-- screen: classic-reading-english -->

- **A familiar text.** When you know what a chapter is about, you can often guess a new word from context instead of reaching for a dictionary.
- **Short pieces.** A verse is one or two sentences, easy to replay and repeat.
- **Audio that matches the text.** Every English translation in the app has a complete recording, and you can follow the words as you listen.

That last point matters. In a study by [Brown, Waring, and Donkaewbua (2008)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ815119.pdf), intermediate learners recognized the meaning of 48% of new words on a multiple-choice test after reading while listening, 45% after reading, and 29% after listening alone. Still, most words were not learned, and words that appeared more often were remembered better. So go through the same passage more than once.

One caveat: Bible English is not everyday English. You'll meet words like disciples and righteousness and few words for daily life, so the Bible works best alongside a course, not instead of one.

## Repeat aloud: pause after every verse
<!-- screen: settings-pause -->

Start simply: a verse plays, and you repeat it aloud during the pause. Once a verse feels familiar, try **shadowing**: speak along with the narrator, a word or two behind. In a study by [Yo Hamada (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168815597504), learners who practiced shadowing perceived English sounds more accurately, while listening comprehension scores improved only in the lower-proficiency group. It helps you hear the language; it is not a shortcut.

How to set it up in Bible Garden:

- **In Classic Reading**, add a pause after every verse: a few seconds, or until you tap Play. A verse plays, you repeat it, you tap Play.
- **In Multi Reading**, add a longer pause after the English reading, 5–8 seconds, so you can repeat without touching the screen. To hear a verse twice, add the same translation as two steps: BSB, a pause, BSB again.
- **If you missed something**, the previous-verse button takes you back to the exact start of the previous verse. The app has no separate repeat button.

For shadowing, choose short verses and start at a slower speed.

## Slow it down: speed and voices
<!-- screen: multi-step-speed -->

For a beginner, the hard part is often keeping up rather than knowing the words. In an experiment by [Yong Zhao (1997)](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/18.1.49), listeners learning English understood more when they controlled the speech rate themselves, and they mostly slowed it down.

The app has four English recordings, each with its own pace. We measured it across each full Bible recording: the translation's word count divided by the audio length (app data as of October 4, 2026).

| Translation | Narrator | Words per minute |
|---|---|---|
| WEB, US edition | Winfred Henson | about 148 |
| BSB | David | about 154 |
| BSB | Bob Souer | about 175 |
| WEBBE, British edition | narrator not credited | about 190 |

What you can adjust:

- **Speed in Classic Reading:** 0.6× to 2.0× in steps of 0.2. At 0.8×, Bob Souer reads at about 140 words per minute.
- **Speed in Multi Reading** is set for each step, from 0.5× to 2.5× in steps of 0.1. There is no single speed button there.
- **One verse twice.** BSB at 0.7×, a pause, then BSB at 1×. Or BSB read by David, then by Bob Souer: the same words in two voices.

## BSB or WEB: which English is easier?
<!-- screen: translation-picker-english -->

Both are modern translations without thee and thou, and both are free of copyright: BSB was [dedicated to the public domain in 2023](https://berean.bible/terms.htm), and [WEB](https://worldenglish.bible/) has been public domain from the start. They still sound different.

| | BSB | WEB |
|---|---|---|
| Basis | A new translation | An update of the 1901 American Standard Version |
| Style | Smooth, contemporary English | More old-fashioned turns of phrase, but more contractions |
| "Behold" in the New Testament | 29 times | 214 times |
| "don’t" in the New Testament | 9 times | 484 times |
| God's name in the Old Testament | Lord | Yahweh in the US edition, LORD in the British |

Compare Matthew 6:34:

- BSB: *Therefore do not worry about tomorrow, for tomorrow will worry about itself. Today has enough trouble of its own.*
- WEB: *Therefore don’t be anxious for tomorrow, for tomorrow will be anxious for itself. Each day’s own evil is sufficient.*

Start with BSB. Once you're comfortable, pair BSB with WEB in Multi Reading to collect synonyms and see different ways to build a sentence. For a wider comparison with KJV, NIV, and other versions, see [Which Bible Translation Is Best?](/articles/which-bible-translation/)

One difference matters in Multi Reading, which pairs verses by position: first with first, second with second. In the Gospels, BSB leaves out 11 verses that are missing from some early manuscripts, such as John 5:4 and Mark 9:44 and 46; WEB keeps them. From that point on, the two translations in a pair drift apart. This happens in 10 of the 89 Gospel chapters. In those chapters, use BSB twice instead of BSB with WEB.

## A 30-day plan
<!-- screen: progress -->

The Gospel of John has 21 chapters, plain language, and the same key words again and again: light, life, truth, believe, love. After it comes 1 John, a short letter with the same vocabulary.

| Days | Read | How |
|---|---|---|
| 1–7 | John 1–7 | Step 1: Multi Reading, BSB then WEB |
| 8–14 | John 8–14 | Steps 1 and 2: the pair, then the same chapter in English only |
| 15–21 | John 15–21 | All three steps, plus 5–10 verses aloud |
| 22–26 | 1 John 1–5 | Listen once with the text; use Multi Reading only if you got lost |
| 27–30 | Your favorite chapters of John | Listen without the text: how much do you understand by ear? |

In John 5, BSB and WEB drift apart, so for that chapter use BSB twice, slow and then at normal speed.

## Frequently asked questions {#faq}

### Can you learn English just by reading the Bible?

No. The Bible gives you a lot of listening material you can understand, but its vocabulary is far from everyday English. A course or textbook covers grammar and daily words; the Bible gives you daily practice.

### What level of English do I need?

It helps to know basic grammar and the most common words; complete beginners should start with a course. Multi Reading lowers the bar: a second wording explains the first, and a slower speed gives you time to catch each word.

### Which Bible translation is best for ESL learners?

Start with the Berean Standard Bible (BSB): smooth modern English and two recordings, by Bob Souer and David. Add the World English Bible (WEB) as a second translation to compare wordings. In Bible Garden, both play verse by verse with the text on screen.

### Is there a Bible in British English?

Yes, the British edition of the World English Bible (WEBBE) uses British spelling. Its narrator is not credited, and at about 190 words per minute it is the fastest English recording in the app, so slow it down at first.

### How can I teach English using the Bible?

Use the same three steps in class: play a verse, pause, and have students repeat it; then compare how BSB and WEB word it. Familiar stories such as the parables work well. Bible Garden is a personal app with no classroom features, so play it from one device through a speaker.

Not yet: Bible Garden runs on iPhone and iPad. A web version for listening on the site and an Android app are in our plans.
