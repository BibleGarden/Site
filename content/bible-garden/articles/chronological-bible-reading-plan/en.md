A chronological Bible reading plan puts the chapters in the order of the events they describe: Job follows Genesis, David's psalms sit beside his story, and the prophets appear next to the kings they spoke to. There is no single agreed chronology, so this order is approximate. We compared three well-known plans and explain our choice wherever they disagree.

**Jump to:** [the 365-day plan](#365-day-chronological-bible-reading-plan) · [the table of eras](#books-of-the-bible-in-chronological-order-a-table-of-eras) · [print the calendar](#365-day-chronological-bible-reading-plan). You can start on any date.

Every chapter is read whole, so events inside a chapter are never rearranged. The placement of Job, many psalms, and several letters is an editorial call. Psalm numbers follow English Bibles.

## Books of the Bible in chronological order: a table of eras

Eight eras run from creation to Revelation. The Days column shows where each era starts and ends in the calendar below; a boundary day contains chapters from both eras.

| Era | Days | What you read | Where the order is uncertain |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Beginnings and patriarchs** | 1–27 | Genesis, Job | Job comes after all of Genesis: no one knows when Job lived or when the book was written |
| **Exodus and wilderness** | 27–76 | Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy; Psalm 90 (a prayer of Moses) before Deuteronomy 33 | Psalm 90 is placed by its heading, not by a known date |
| **Conquest and judges** | 76–93 | Joshua, Judges, Ruth | The last chapters of Judges (17–21) describe an earlier time |
| **Saul, David, and Solomon** | 94–152 | Genealogies in 1 Chronicles 1–8; 1–2 Samuel, 1 Kings 1–11, and the parallel chapters of 1–2 Chronicles; David's psalms beside the events; the remaining Psalms 1–71; Proverbs 1–24, Song of Solomon, Psalm 72, Ecclesiastes | Psalms 1–71 form one block of the collection, not a set of dates; the wisdom books are linked to Solomon by tradition |
| **Divided kingdom and prophets** | 153–214 | 1 Kings 12–22, 2 Kings 1–23, 2 Chronicles 10–35; Jonah, Amos, Hosea, Isaiah, Micah, Nahum, Zephaniah, Habakkuk, the early chapters of Jeremiah; Proverbs 25–31 | Isaiah 40–66 follows chapters 36–39 directly; the plan makes no claim about when they were written |

## 365-day chronological Bible reading plan
<!-- screen: classic-reading -->

The calendar covers all 1,189 chapters of the 66 books, each one once. That averages three to four chapters a day, but a day can have anywhere from 1 to 14: days with short chapters get more of them. The days are balanced by the length of Bob Souer's Berean Standard Bible (BSB) recording, so each one takes between 8 and 14½ minutes with that narrator, about 11 on average. Other recordings run a little longer or shorter.

A day can have up to five readings. Day 105, for example, is 1 Samuel 21, Psalm 34, Psalm 56, 1 Samuel 22, Psalm 57: David on the run from Saul, with the psalms whose headings point to those moments.

Pick a start date to fill in the dates. The Print button prepares the calendar for paper; to get a PDF, choose "Save as PDF" in the print dialog. Check off each day on the printed page.

## How chronological order differs from book order
<!-- screen: classic-reading-psalm -->

Printed Bibles group the books by type: law, history, poetry, prophets. That's why Psalms sits in the middle of the Old Testament and the prophets come after all the historical books, even though they spoke during the reigns described in 1–2 Kings. A chronological plan braids these threads together:

- **A psalm next to its story.** Psalm 51 shares a day with 2 Samuel 11–12, the story of David and Bathsheba. Its heading mentions the prophet Nathan's visit.
- **A prophet next to his king.** Isaiah 7 opens "in the days of Ahaz," so the plan places it after Ahaz's story in 2 Kings 16.
- **Two accounts of the same events.** Chronicles alternates with the parallel chapters of Samuel and Kings, and the four Gospels alternate with each other.
- **A letter next to its city.** 1–2 Thessalonians follow Acts 15–18, where Paul plants the church in Thessalonica.

The trade-off is real. You switch books often, the same events come up two to four times in a row, and the Gospels don't start until day 275. A chronological plan works best for readers who have been through the Bible before. For a first full read-through, we suggest the parallel plan in [How to Read the Bible in a Year](/articles/bible-in-a-year/); if the Bible is new to you, start with the [seven-day plan](/articles/how-to-start-reading-the-bible/). Other well-known plans, including M'Cheyne and The Bible Recap's chronological year, are compared in [Famous Bible Reading Plans Compared](/articles/popular-bible-reading-plans/).

## How we built the plan, and where the order is debated

We compared three complete one-year plans: [Blue Letter Bible](https://www.blueletterbible.org/assets/pdf/dbrp/1Yr_ChronologicalPlan.pdf), [Back to the Bible (2025)](https://bttbfiles.com/eResources/2025%20Bible%20reading%20plans/2025_BRP_Chronological.pdf), and the Tyndale chronology behind the NLT One Year Chronological Bible, published by [Bible In A Year Online](https://bibleinayearonline.com/readingplan/chronologicalbibleinayearreadingplan.pdf). We also checked the Gospels against A. T. Robertson's *A Harmony of the Gospels* ([1922](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/36264/36264-h/36264-h.htm)). Where the plans agree, we follow the shared outline: Genesis, the exodus, the kingdoms, the exile, the Gospels, Acts. Where they differ, headings and dates in the biblical text decide; in the disputed Old Testament spots we most often went with the Tyndale/NLT option. We didn't borrow anyone's daily schedule: the days are calculated from audio length.

We don't hide the overlap with our sources. The first two plans share much of their outline, so their agreement isn't two independent votes. Our order matches Blue Letter Bible over long stretches, such as Exodus through Deuteronomy 32, because that is the common historical backbone with nothing to rearrange. Every disputed decision, its alternatives, and the overlaps with sources are recorded in our [decision log](https://github.com/BibleGarden/Site/blob/main/tools/data/chronological-decisions.md).

The main judgment calls:

- **Job** comes after Genesis, before Exodus. Two of the plans put him after Genesis 11; we keep Genesis unbroken because Job's date is unknown.
- **Psalms.** When a heading names an event, the psalm sits beside it: Psalms 34 and 56 with David among the Philistines (1 Samuel 21), Psalms 57 and 142 in the cave, Psalm 3 during the flight from Absalom. Psalm 72, "of Solomon," follows the temple dedication. The rest fall into three blocks: Psalms 1–71 after David, Psalms 73–89 and 137 ("By the rivers of Babylon") beside Lamentations, Psalms 91–150 after Nehemiah. These mark places in the collection, not dates of writing.
- **Chronicles.** The genealogies of 1 Chronicles 1–8 come before 1 Samuel; chapter 9, a list of Jerusalem's residents after the exile, sits between Nehemiah 11 and 12.
- **Proverbs 25–31** comes in Hezekiah's reign: Proverbs 25:1 says "the men of Hezekiah" copied the proverbs of chapters 25–29. Chapters 30–31, the words of Agur and Lemuel, stay alongside as an editorial choice; their date is unknown.
- **Jeremiah and Daniel** are reordered by the dates given at the start of chapters. Jeremiah 24, for example, follows Jehoiachin's exile in 2 Kings 24.
- **Joel and Obadiah** carry no date. We place Obadiah after the fall of Jerusalem, since Edom gloats over Judah's disaster, and Joel late, before Malachi, as an editorial choice. Two of the plans put both much earlier.
- **Gospels.** Whole chapters can't produce an exact harmony, so some episodes come a little before or after their place.
- **Letters.** James follows Acts 12 and Galatians follows Acts 14, before the Jerusalem Council: this reflects an early dating, not a proven fact. Romans follows 2 Corinthians, before Acts 20. Hebrews follows 2 Timothy; its author and date are unknown. Revelation comes last.

## Multi Reading and pauses
<!-- screen: multi-reading -->

A chronological plan sets story and prayer side by side: first the event, then the psalm whose heading ties it to those days. A psalm is worth slowing down for. In Bible Garden you can add a pause after each verse, paragraph, or section, for a few seconds or until you tap Play.

**Multi Reading** plays each verse in several translations in a row, with a pause between them: BSB with Bob Souer, for example, then WEB with Winfred Henson. You can mix languages too, with the same verse shown in the other translation under each one; see [Bilingual Bible App](/articles/bible-in-two-languages/). Multi Reading doesn't move on to the next chapter by itself, so no extra chapter starts. Two translations take about twice as long, though, so use it for a short passage, such as the day's psalm in two English translations. For Psalms, stick to English pairs: the Russian translations and Ukrainian NPU number most psalms one lower, and Multi Reading doesn't adjust for that yet. To see how long a reading takes at your pace, try the [calculator](/articles/how-long-to-read-the-bible/).

## Frequently asked questions {#faq}

### What is a chronological Bible reading plan?

It's a plan that puts the chapters in the order of the events they describe rather than the order of the books: Job after Genesis, psalms beside David's story, prophets beside their kings, letters beside Acts. The order is approximate, since many dates are debated and a single chapter can cover events from different times. Our plan covers all 1,189 chapters in 365 days.

### Can I print the plan or save it as a PDF?

Yes. Choose a start date and press Print above the calendar. To get a file, pick "Save as PDF" instead of a printer in the print dialog. There is no separate download.

### Is the order of events the same as the order the books were written?

No. The plan follows the time of the events described. Books may have been written or compiled later: Chronicles retells the history of the kings after the exile, and the Psalms were gathered over centuries. A book's place in the plan is not its date of writing.

### How long does the plan take each day?

Between 8 and 14½ minutes of audio with Bob Souer's BSB recording, about 11 on average, or three to four chapters if you read. Other narrators take a little more or less. Leave extra time for the days that bring several books together.

### Is a chronological plan good for a first read-through?

It works, but it's harder than reading in book order: you jump between books a lot, and if you start on January 1, the Gospels don't begin until early October. For a first read-through, we suggest reading the Old and New Testaments in parallel.

### Why doesn't the plan include the Catholic and Orthodox books?

The plan covers the 66 books shared by all Christian traditions, the same ones recorded in the app. The deuterocanonical books and the Greek additions to Esther and Daniel are not included.
